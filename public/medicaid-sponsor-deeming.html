Sponsor Deeming and Medicaid: How a Sponsor's Income Counts Against an Application

Many sponsored immigrants are surprised when a Medicaid application is denied even though their own income is low. Here's why, and what exceptions exist.

Here's the short answer. If someone signed a legally binding Affidavit of Support (Form I-864) to sponsor a family member's green card, federal law can require that sponsor's income and resources to be counted — "deemed" — as if they belonged to the sponsored immigrant when a benefit program decides eligibility. This applies to Medicaid and CHIP for lawful permanent residents with a qualifying sponsor, under the Immigration and Nationality Act. That's how a household with modest income can still be denied: on paper, the sponsor's income pushes the applicant "over the limit," even if the sponsor isn't actually providing that support.

This is complex, and rules vary by state. CMS gives states several allowed approaches to sponsor deeming for Medicaid and lets them combine methods, so the details differ from state to state. Ask your state Medicaid agency exactly how it applies deeming and what exceptions it recognizes. Find your agency in our State Medicaid directory.

Who this applies to

Sponsor deeming for Medicaid and CHIP applies to lawful permanent residents whose sponsor signed the current, legally enforceable Affidavit of Support (Form I-864). If your sponsor signed an older or different form, such as Form I-134, the sponsor-deeming rules generally don't apply the same way. Some categories of immigrants, including refugees and certain other humanitarian categories, are excluded from sponsor deeming entirely.

Why it exists: the affidavit of support

When someone sponsors a family member for a green card, they sign Form I-864, a legally enforceable promise to financially support that person. Because the sponsor made that commitment to the government as a condition of the immigrant's admission, programs like Medicaid are allowed to treat the sponsor's income and resources as available to the immigrant, whether or not the sponsor is actually sending money.

Sponsors can also be billed back

The same law that allows deeming also makes sponsors financially responsible after the fact. Sponsors who sign the I-864 can be required to reimburse the state for the cost of most benefits, including Medicaid, that their sponsored immigrant receives. That reimbursement obligation is separate from, and in addition to, deeming during the eligibility decision itself.

How long deeming lasts

For SSI, sponsor deeming generally runs until the sponsored immigrant becomes a U.S. citizen or has worked 40 qualifying quarters — but as a general duration rule, affidavits signed on or after October 1, 1996 lead to a deeming period of about 3 years (36 months) from the date of lawful admission. That figure comes from SSI rules specifically; Medicaid deeming periods and their exceptions are set by each state under the flexibility CMS gives them, so confirm the duration and any cutoff with your state Medicaid agency rather than assuming the SSI timeline applies.

An exception worth knowing: the indigence exception (SSI)

For SSI, there's a recognized "indigence exception" that can pause deeming for 12 months at a time if, even after any actual support from the sponsor, the immigrant can't get both food and shelter. Generally, the immigrant must be living apart from the sponsor to qualify, since living together is treated as evidence the sponsor is providing food and shelter. This exception is documented for SSI. If you believe a similar hardship exception should apply to a Medicaid case, ask your state Medicaid agency directly — states aren't required to mirror the SSI rule exactly.

Sponsor deeming and Emergency Medicaid

Emergency Medicaid follows a separate federal eligibility path built around treating an emergency medical condition, and it isn't limited to people with a qualifying immigration status the way regular Medicaid is. See Emergency Medicaid for how that coverage works. Because sponsor deeming rules are set state by state for Medicaid generally, ask your state agency specifically how (or whether) it applies deeming to an emergency-only application.

What to do if you think deeming is affecting your case

  1. Ask the eligibility worker to explain the denial. Request the specific reason and whether sponsor income was counted.
  2. Find out which affidavit your sponsor signed. Deeming generally applies to the current Form I-864, not older or different forms.
  3. Ask about hardship or indigence exceptions in your state. Some states recognize exceptions similar to the SSI indigence exception; ask specifically since this isn't automatic.
  4. Ask how long deeming will apply. Confirm the end date or triggering event your state uses.
  5. If you're denied, you can appeal. Medicaid decisions come with appeal rights. See Your Rights.

Immigration and public-benefits rules interact in complicated ways, and getting it wrong can affect an immigration case. Consider talking with an immigration attorney or an accredited representative. Common questions

What is sponsor deeming?

It's counting a sponsor's income and resources as if they belonged to the sponsored immigrant when a program decides eligibility. It applies because the sponsor signed a legally enforceable Affidavit of Support (Form I-864) promising to support the immigrant.

Does sponsor deeming apply to Medicaid?

Yes. Federal law requires states to count a qualifying sponsor's income and resources when deciding a sponsored lawful permanent resident's Medicaid or CHIP eligibility, though CMS lets each state choose among several methods for how it applies deeming.

Does deeming apply if the applicant needs Emergency Medicaid?

Emergency Medicaid has its own separate eligibility path that doesn't depend on regular immigration-status rules. Ask your state Medicaid agency how it treats sponsor income for an emergency-only application, since the details are set state by state.

Is my sponsor responsible for paying back Medicaid I received?

Under federal law, sponsors who sign the I-864 affidavit can be held liable for reimbursing the cost of most means-tested benefits, including Medicaid, that their sponsored immigrant receives.

Does deeming last forever?

No. For SSI, deeming generally ends 3 years after the immigrant's lawful admission for affidavits signed since October 1996. Medicaid deeming periods are set by each state, so ask your state Medicaid agency how long deeming applies in your case.
